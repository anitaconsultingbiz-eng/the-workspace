Project: The Experience Vault

Why I created it, how the idea developed, and the decisions behind the working system.

01 / The challenge

After more than 20 years in higher education operations, I knew I had handled complex situations, solved problems, improved processes, and worked across departments. But when interview questions asked for a specific example, I found myself struggling to retrieve the right story. Sitting at my computer trying to remember everything was frustrating. I needed a process that worked with how memories actually come back to me.

02 / Where it started

I began speaking about my experiences using ChatGPT's voice feature, then found iPhone Voice Memos especially useful. I could capture a memory while walking around, cooking, or being outside, without stopping to sit at my computer. Later, I transferred those spoken notes into ChatGPT and used the conversations to reflect, ask myself questions, and recognize experiences I might otherwise have overlooked. The first deliverable was not software: it was a growing body of knowledge about my own work.

03 / The idea: a bank of experience

I wanted a system that would keep growing. The idea of a vault came from treating each remembered experience as a deposit. I could preserve the complete story, organize it by topic, and later withdraw the most relevant example when preparing for a particular job. I did not want to rewrite or lose my original words every time I adapted an answer to a different opportunity.

04 / Turning the idea into requirements

Through extensive back-and-forth conversations with ChatGPT, I worked out the structure and features: a searchable Knowledge Bank; Make a Deposit; Job Match Studio; saved opportunities; suggested questions for gaps; full original answers; printable preparation reports; and a connection to the source Google Doc. I made decisions about how the interface should work, what language it should use, and what information needed to be retained.

05 / How I developed and tested it

I used ChatGPT to help write and revise Google Apps Script, HTML, CSS, and JavaScript, then implemented and tested the changes myself. When a saved answer failed to update, a report had awkward page breaks, or a workflow was confusing, I returned to the code and revised the behavior. This was an iterative collaboration built around requirements, testing, and corrections—not a single generated application.

06 / How it works

Google Sheets holds the working knowledge records. Google Docs holds the original source document. Google Apps Script connects the data and interface. Job Match Studio compares pasted responsibilities with stored experiences using rule-based matching, explains why results are relevant, and supports interview preparation without requiring a paid AI API.

07 / Design choices

Preserve full answers instead of overwriting them. Make it easy to deposit new experiences as they come to mind. Keep source and working copies distinct. Allow more than one useful example for a responsibility. Make the system private, affordable, and practical for repeated use.

08 / Real-world testing and results

I used the Experience Vault approach to prepare for an actual job interview. I worked from the responsibilities in the posting, identified relevant experiences in my knowledge bank, and developed questions to practice. During the interview, I found that the questions I was asked were very close to the questions I had prepared for—approximately 99% similar by my own estimate. This was not a formal accuracy test, and I cannot know how the interviewers created their questions. What mattered was that the preparation helped me retrieve examples, organize my thinking, and feel ready to respond. It showed me that the system was useful beyond the development stage.

09 / What this project demonstrates

I translated a personal challenge into a structured workflow and then a working tool. It brings together reflection, knowledge management, process design, requirements development, AI-assisted coding, troubleshooting, and practical automation. The Experience Vault is an independent personal application, not a deployed employer system.

Grant Management Dashboard

The operational need, design decisions, and scope of the working prototype.

The challenge

A senior grant manager needed a clearer way to monitor a portfolio spanning four centers and multiple grants. The important questions were practical: Which grants are spending quickly? Which may be running out of money? Where might a no-cost extension be needed? How do salary allocations affect the picture? What requests from principal investigators still need follow-up?

My approach

I started with the questions the grant manager needed to answer, then worked backward to the information and views that would support those decisions. Rather than build a collection of unrelated charts, I organized the prototype as a path from a portfolio overview to center and grant details, with a separate way to capture and revisit PI requests.

What I developed

A Google Sheets and Google Apps Script dashboard prototype with an administrative portfolio view, center and grant detail, financial indicators, personnel and effort information, and a request workflow with history. The interface uses demonstration data to illustrate how the views fit together.

Design decisions

I prioritized burn-rate visibility, remaining funds, and the ability to identify grants that may require attention. I also included room for personnel allocations, request comments, and printable reporting. Each view had to answer a specific operational question rather than simply display more information.

How the scope changed

The initial concept included separate workspaces for four centers. Those center-specific workspaces were paused because of policy considerations, while the administrative portfolio view remained the focus. This was an important scope decision: the prototype needed to respect the actual working environment, not just what was technically possible.

Development and testing

I used Google Sheets as the data foundation and Google Apps Script to build the web interface. I tested the demonstration views and verified that PI request history could be retained. This work was iterative, shaped by the grant manager's operational priorities and feedback.

What this work demonstrates

Requirements discovery, operational process mapping, dashboard design, data organization, iterative development, and translating stakeholder needs into usable views. This is a client-informed prototype using sample data—not a live production deployment, an audited financial report, or a claim of full pre-award or post-award grants administration.

Project: Advancement Services Simulation

A fictional gift-processing environment built to practice reviewing transactions, identifying discrepancies, and documenting decisions before a batch can move forward.

The operational question

How do you know a gift batch is ready to process?

In advancement services, accurate gift records depend on more than totals. Individual transactions, payment status, duplicates, and processor records need to agree. I created a practice environment to work through those checks and understand the decisions behind them.

Start with an assigned review

Understand the assignment

The workspace introduces the gift-intake review and gives the reviewer a defined task. I wanted the simulation to begin with the same question an operations team would ask: what needs checking before this batch can be cleared?

Reconcile the batch and examine individual records

Look beyond the summary

The intake screen compares settled rows, distinct transaction IDs, intake amounts, and processor controls. A batch can appear consistent by one measure and still contain a duplicate or a dollar variance.

In the demonstration, the duplicate transaction creates an extra settled row and a $750 difference—evidence that calls for investigation, not automatic correction.

Make the issue visible and traceable

Move from discrepancy to action

The simulation includes a work queue and review workflow so a questionable record can be held, categorized, and followed up instead of silently passing through the process.

Keep an operational record

Document the decision

Review logs and operational reporting make the work traceable. The goal is not simply to find an error, but to show what was reviewed, what remains unresolved, and what action should follow.

What this simulation demonstrates

Translating an unfamiliar operational area into a structured practice workflow: intake → comparison → exception review → documented decision. All names, transactions, and amounts are fictional; this is not a live fundraising system.

Advancement Services Simulation

Learning the operational controls behind accurate gift processing.

Why I developed it

As I explored advancement services as a possible career direction, I wanted more than a description of the field. I wanted to understand what the back-end work actually involves: how gift records are received, checked, held for review, and documented. I built a fictional workflow to practice those responsibilities hands-on.

The problem I modeled

A batch contains multiple intake rows, including a duplicate settled transaction. That creates a difference between the intake amount and the processor control. A reviewer must identify the specific issue rather than rely on a total or assume that matching counts mean every record is correct.

My approach

I broke the work into manageable decisions: review the assignment, reconcile batch totals, compare transaction-level details, flag exceptions, and document what needs follow-up. This made it possible to practice the reasoning behind the process instead of memorizing terminology.

What I built

A browser-based practice workspace with an assignment brief, gift-intake reconciliation, transaction comparison, a work queue, constituent records, a review log, and an operations report. The fictional data intentionally includes a duplicate transaction and a $750 variance to create a meaningful review exercise.

Why the controls matter

Summary measures help direct attention, but they do not make a decision for the reviewer. I wanted the simulation to distinguish between finding an exception and deciding how to resolve it, with a clear record of the reasoning.

What I learned

This exercise helped me connect familiar operations skills—data checks, documentation, process controls, and exception management—to advancement services. It was a way to explore the work through a realistic scenario before claiming direct professional experience in that specialty.

What this work demonstrates

Self-directed learning, process mapping, data validation, reconciliation thinking, exception handling, and the design of a practical learning simulation.
